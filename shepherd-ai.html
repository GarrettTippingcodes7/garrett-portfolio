Case study · No. 3

Shepherd AI

Designing an AI pricing tool for independent car dealers, around a pricing model that didn’t exist yet.

Role
Sole UX/UI Designer
Tools
Figma, Claude
Timeline
Spring 2024

Context

Large dealer groups price inventory with enterprise tools and live auction data. Independent lots usually can’t afford those, so they price from book values, gut feel, and hours of manual comp research. Shepherd AI wanted to close that gap with DriveFlow.

I was the only designer on the project and owned all of the UX: the landing page, the conversational intake, the price report, the team dashboard, mobile, error flows, and the copy. I did it on a compressed timeline while working full-time as a gas utility technician.

What I was designing around

Constraint

An unproven model

The pricing model was being built at the same time I was designing. Data-licensing limits meant no one knew how accurate it would be.

Constraint

A moving scope

Requirements shifted as the model and the company’s vision changed. What I designed had to survive those shifts.

Constraint

No direct users

I never spoke with a dealer. Everything I knew about users came secondhand, through a stakeholder.

I want to be upfront about that last one. I never researched or tested with dealers. The feedback I did get came from checkpoint walkthroughs with the team, where the PM and a teammate clicked through the prototype and flagged problems in onboarding and messaging. The decisions on this page are judgment calls built on that, stakeholder input, and the dealer workflow I mapped, not validated findings.

Where pricing breaks

I started by mapping how a dealer gets a car from acquisition to an online listing: 16 steps, from inspection and reconditioning to photos and marketing. Only four of them actually set the price: market research, cost analysis, margin, and the final list price. That’s where DriveFlow aimed. With no dealer access, I built the map from desk research, a lot of it AI-assisted, and treated it as a working model, not a fact.

The highest-pressure moment is the trade-in appraisal. A customer is standing on the lot, and the dealer needs a number that wins the deal without giving away margin. That’s where DriveFlow had to be fast and believable.

Designing for trust with an unproven model

Every major decision came back to one question: what is this product worth to a dealer if the AI’s number is wrong? My first answer is in my notes from the time: ask for the dealer’s location up front and show the price range for similar cars in their area, so they get real value “before machine learning happens.”

Problem:

A single number nobody could verify

If the report opened on one predicted price and that price missed, dealers would stop trusting the tool after the first bad appraisal.

Solution:

Lead with the value graph

The report leads with a draggable value graph instead of the predicted price. It shows where the car started, where similar vehicles in the dealer’s area sit now, and where the model thinks it’s headed over the next year. Dealers can scrub through it and check it against what they already know. The predicted price sits inside that range, and Shepherd narrows it with each answer instead of announcing one number up front.

Problem:

A backend that kept changing

The model’s inputs changed as the data situation changed. A fixed intake form would have needed a redesign every time.

Solution:

Modular intake blocks

I built the conversational intake from self-contained prompt blocks: location, mileage, condition, trim and add-ons. Each block carried its own input and its own error states, like “Springfield, MS or MA?” or a mileage of a million. A first block of required questions was enough for the model to run; an optional second block let dealers keep refining. Blocks could be added, removed, or reordered without rebuilding the flow.

Problem:

Skeptical buyers

Dealers are wary of new tech, and a typical landing page asks them to take claims on faith.

Solution:

Make the page the product

The landing page runs the product itself: the VIN field sits in the hero, and an AI conversation on the left drives the price report on the right. Dealers see it work before they sign up for early access. At the first checkpoint, the team asked whether the split screen was needed at all, or whether chat could shrink to a floating bubble. I kept the split. Watching the price move as you answer is what makes the number believable, and a bubble hides that.

From pricing tool to team dashboard

Midway through, DriveFlow grew from a single-user pricing tool into a multi-department team dashboard. That came from a mix of real discovery, a growing vision from the stakeholder, and some scope creep. There wasn’t one clean cause.

To scope 2.0, I mapped the five departments of a used-car dealership (sales, finance, inventory, service, and admin) and what each would need from a shared vehicle report. The rule I wrote down: at a small lot where one person covers three of those roles, the system still has to make sense. I also designed mobile and the error flows, which I put first on the MVP list. The expansion was the right direction, but it meant more surface area for one designer on a tight timeline.

The pivot

Before development started, Shepherd AI pivoted to a different market and DriveFlow was shelved. The design was complete and structured for a Figma-to-Webflow handoff, but it never shipped. There are no usage numbers, so this case study is about decisions, not outcomes.

What I’d carry forward

Designing around an unproven model taught me to ask what a product is worth when the AI is wrong, and to design that version first. The value graph came out of that question.

If I did it again, I’d push for even two or three conversations with dealers before locking the intake. Secondhand insight got me a plausible design, not a tested one. The first thing I’d build to check the model is the sold list I sketched for 2.0: predicted price next to what each car actually sold for.

I’d also name scope trade-offs out loud sooner. Saying yes to the team dashboard fit the company’s vision, but every new screen meant less depth on the ones that mattered most.
